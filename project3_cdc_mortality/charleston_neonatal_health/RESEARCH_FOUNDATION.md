# Neonatal and Infant Health in Charleston, South Carolina: A Data-Backed Research Foundation

**Geography:** Charleston County, SC (FIPS 45019)  
**Primary data years:** 2023 (SC DPH Vital Metrics Summary), 2024 (March of Dimes PeriStats)  
**Prepared:** August 2026  
**Use:** Foundation for investigative and academic work on perinatal inequity in the Lowcountry

---

## Abstract

Charleston County presents a paradox familiar in American metropolitan health: aggregate county indicators—life expectancy 77.9 years, median household income $88,111—mask deep stratification in who survives and thrives in the first year of life. In 2024, **1 in 10 babies** (10.5%, 537 births) was born preterm, earning a **D+** on the March of Dimes report card. Low birthweight affects **9.1%** of births (2023), but the burden is not shared equally: **17.3%** of Black infants versus **6.1%** of White infants were low birthweight—a **2.8-fold disparity**. Infant mortality rose nearly **48%** between 2013 and 2023 by PeriStats linked data, with Black infants dying at **13.7 per 1,000** live births compared with **5.3** for White infants (2021–2023 average). Roughly **14.5%** of births followed inadequate prenatal care; **50.3%** of births were paid by Medicaid; only **34.6%** of mothers received WIC during pregnancy despite widespread food and housing insecurity at the tract level. This document synthesizes every major factor domain—clinical outcomes, access, nutrition, insurance, environment, structural racism, and hospital capacity—with a citation for each quantitative claim.

## 1. Introduction and scope

### 1.1 Why neonatal health matters

Neonatal mortality (death within 0–27 days) and infant mortality (death within the first year) are sentinel indicators of population health, health-system performance, and social equity (CDC, 2024). Preterm birth and low birthweight are the leading preventable drivers of neonatal death nationally, accounting for roughly **35.7%** of infant deaths in South Carolina when grouped as preterm-related (March of Dimes prematurity profile, 2024).

Charleston is the **third-largest birthing county** in South Carolina by volume (~4,856 resident live births in 2023; ~5,094 in 2024) and hosts the state's only **Level IV** neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at MUSC Children's Health. Yet regional prosperity coexists with tract-level poverty, housing cost burden, and racial gaps that predict worse birth outcomes.

## 2. Core birth outcomes

### 2.1 Preterm birth

**Definition:** Live birth before 37 completed weeks gestation (March of Dimes / NCHS).

| Indicator | Charleston | SC | HP2030 target | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preterm birth rate (2024) | **10.5%** (537 births) | 11.6% | 9.4% | [March of Dimes Report Card](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/reports/south-carolina/report-card) |
| Report card grade | **D+** | F (43rd of 52) | — | Same |
| Very preterm (<32 wk, 2024) | 1.6% | 1.8% (state) | — | [PeriStats preterm profile](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?creg=45019&top=3&stop=55) |
| Moderately preterm (32–36 wk) | 9.0% | 9.9% (state) | — | Same |

**Interpretation:** Charleston performs **better than the state** on preterm birth but **fails the Healthy People 2030 target** of 9.4%. At current volume (~5,100 births/year), each 1-percentage-point reduction in preterm birth would spare roughly **51** babies from prematurity-associated morbidity annually.

### 2.2 Low birthweight (LBW)

**Definition:** Birthweight < 2,500 grams (5 lb 8 oz).

| Group | LBW count | LBW % | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| All births (2023) | 442 / 4,856 | **9.1%** | VMS Table C-27 |
| Non-Hispanic White | 178 / 2,901 | **6.1%** | VMS Table C-27 |
| Non-Hispanic Black | 200 / 1,154 | **17.3%** | VMS Table C-27 |
| Hispanic | 64 / 801 | **8.0%** | VMS Table C-27 |
| Very low birthweight (<1,500 g) | 48 | **1.0%** | VMS Table C-29 |

March of Dimes reports **9.2%** LBW for 2024 and a **Black/White ratio of ~2.9** (18.9% vs 6.6%, 2021–2023 average) — [PeriStats birthweight](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?creg=45019&top=4&stop=75).

**Equity finding:** Black infants are approximately **three times** as likely to be born low birthweight. LBW overlaps with preterm birth but also reflects **intrauterine growth restriction**, chronic maternal stress, hypertension, and nutrition deficits.

## 3. Prenatal care and healthcare access

### 3.1 Timing and adequacy of prenatal care

March of Dimes (2024, Charleston):

| Category | Percent | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Early prenatal care | 78.7% | [PeriStats PNC](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?creg=45019&top=5&stop=105) |
| Second trimester start | 15.1% | Same |
| Late or no prenatal care | **6.2%** (~1 in 16 births) | Same |
| Adequate/adequate plus (Kotelchuck) | **80.0%** | Same |
| Inadequate prenatal care | **14.5%** (~1 in 7 births) | Same |
| Intermediate care | 5.6% | Same |

**Healthy People 2030 target:** 80.5% adequate/adequate plus — Charleston is **borderline**.

VMS 2023 Kotelchuck index by race (Table C-11A):

| Race | Inadequate | Adequate + Adequate Plus |
|---|---|---|
| White (n=1,154) | 16.9% (195) | 44.2% (510) |
| Black (n=801) | **36.6%** (293) | 23.6% (189) |

Black birthing people were **2.2×** as likely to receive inadequate prenatal care by Kotelchuck index—a structural access and quality gap, not merely a behavior gap.

### 3.2 Insurance and payment source

VMS 2023, expected pay source at delivery (Table C-35):

| Pay source | All races | White | Black |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicaid | **50.3%** | 26.9% | **70.3%** |
| Private insurance | 23.9% | 67.6% | 16.6% |
| Self-pay | 22.6% | 16.6% | 4.9% |

**22.6% self-pay** among all births signals financial barriers and potential charity-care dependence—a risk for care delays. Statewide, **43.1%** of mothers were on Medicaid at birth in 2024 (March of Dimes Report Card); Charleston exceeds that at **50.3%** (2023 VMS).

South Carolina **expanded Medicaid postpartum coverage to 12 months** (2023 policy)—a critical intervention for the **60%** of SC births on Medicaid, but expansion does not resolve **pre-conception** and **first-trimester** access gaps.

| Facility | Role | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **MUSC Health** | SC's only **Level IV** NICU; ECMO; regional referral center | [MUSC NICU](https://musckids.org/health-care-services/childrens-health/neonatal-intensive-care) |
| Roper St. Francis | High-performing maternity (U.S. News) | U.S. News hospital rankings |

**Equity question for research:** Concentration of the highest acuity care at MUSC creates a **two-tier geography**—proximity to downtown Charleston vs North Charleston, rural Dorchester/Berkeley corridors, and islands. Transportation barriers (see Section 6) compound this.

## 4. Nutrition, WIC, and maternal metabolic health

### 4.1 WIC participation

| Measure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Mothers receiving WIC during pregnancy | 1,678 (**34.6%**) | VMS Table C-34 |
| SC statewide WIC participation rate (approx.) | ~53% of eligible pregnancies served | USDA FNS / state reports |

**Gap:** An estimated **majority of income-eligible pregnant people may not receive WIC** despite Medicaid enrollment above 50%. WIC provides **nutritious foods, breastfeeding support, and referrals**—evidence-based for reducing LBW and prematurity (USDA, Cochrane reviews).

### 4.2 Maternal BMI and obesity

VMS Table C-36 (maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, 2023):

| Category | White births | Black births |
|---|---|---|
| Obese | **39.0%** | **39.0%** |
| Overweight | 23.6% | 23.6% |
| Normal | 33.5% | 33.5% |
| Underweight | 3.6% | 3.6% |

Obesity prevalence near **40%** elevates risk of **gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, and stillbirth**. Charleston mirrors national racial convergence on obesity with divergent **downstream** outcomes—consistent with **weathering** and structural racism hypotheses (Geronimus, 1992).

## 6. Environment, housing, and climate vulnerability

### 6.1 Air quality

| Pollutant | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| PM2.5 annual mean | **8.4 µg/m³** | County Health Rankings 2025 |

PM2.5 exposure in pregnancy is associated with **preterm birth and low birthweight** (EPA/NIEHS systematic reviews).

### 6.2 Housing and climate

Charleston County faces **sea-level rise, flooding, and extreme heat** (Charleston County Multi-Hazard Vulnerability Assessment, 2023). Housing cost burden (**34.2%** severe housing problems) forces tradeoffs between **rent, food, and prenatal care co-pays**.

Tract-level PLACES measures document:
- **Housing insecurity:** up to ~40%+ in high-SVI tracts
- **Utility shut-off threat:** concentrated inland
- **Transportation barriers:** strongest in rural western tracts

These are **neonatal-relevant** because they affect **stress, care attendance, and heat exposure** during pregnancy.

## 9. Racial equity and structural injustice — synthesis

The data converge on a coherent equity narrative:

1. **Black infants** face **2.8–3×** higher LBW and **2.6×** higher infant mortality (3-yr PeriStats averages) despite similar obesity prevalence.
2. **Black birthing people** are **2.2×** more likely to receive **inadequate** prenatal care and **2.6×** more likely to deliver on **Medicaid**.
3. **73.1%** of Black births are to unmarried mothers vs **14.1%** among White births—reflecting **economic and partner-support gaps**, not moral failure.
4. Tract-level **SVI, food insecurity, and transportation barriers** map onto historically **redlined** and **industrial** corridors (North Charleston, portions of West Ashley and rural zones)—see companion `charleston_county` tract analysis.

This pattern matches **weathering**, **obstetric racism**, and **two-tier hospital systems** documented nationally (CDC HEAR, ACOG committees, March of Dimes equity reports).

## 12. Conclusion

Charleston County is **not** a monolith of healthy births. County-level preterm and infant mortality compare favorably to South Carolina, but **trends are worsening**, **racial disparities are large**, **one in seven** pregnancies receives inadequate prenatal care, and **half** of births rely on Medicaid in a state with persistent uninsurance and housing stress. The region's Level IV NICU capacity saves the highest-acuity newborns while **upstream prevention**—WIC, early prenatal care, environmental justice, and obstetric equity—remains under-resourced.

This package provides the **cited evidentiary foundation** for investigative journalism, IRB-ready research proposals, and community advocacy. The next step is primary data: hospital-level C-section and complication rates stratified by race, ZIP code, and payer; qualitative interviews with Black birthing people in North Charleston; and geospatial linkage of flood/heat exposure to birth outcomes.
